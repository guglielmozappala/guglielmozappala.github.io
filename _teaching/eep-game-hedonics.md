**Week 9, Friday 13 November.** About 35 to 40 minutes, played in six teams.

## The story

Seventy-five households choose where to live among six neighbourhoods, A to F. The neighbourhoods differ in environmental quality (EQ) and school quality (SQ), and the price of a house depends on how many households move in. Your team plays one household type, and every type values environmental quality differently. Round by round you work out where the households end up and what each neighbourhood costs. Sorting, and a price gradient, emerge from your answers.

- Six teams, one phone per team, join with a team name. Household types are assigned in the order teams join.

## How we play

- Each household gets utility U = α · EQ + β · SQ − P from living in a neighbourhood with environmental quality EQ, school quality SQ and price P. Your team's page shows your type's α and β and each round's EQ and SQ by neighbourhood.
- **Supply rule.** Neighbourhood A is free: P = 0 whatever happens. In B to F the price equals the number of houses occupied there.
- **Each round** your team submits three things: how the 75 households split across A to F in equilibrium, the neighbourhood your own type ends up in, and the utility it gets there. The app checks each part and tells you which is right. You have three attempts per round; after the third wrong submission the round's answer is revealed to your team.
- Rounds 1 to 3: every household type has the same preferences, and the neighbourhoods differ first in EQ only, then in EQ and SQ. Rounds 4 and 5: the types differ in how much they value environmental quality, and only some neighbourhoods are clean.
- A round's market is revealed to everyone once every team is resolved.

## Tips

- An equilibrium is a split where no household wants to move. Check it neighbourhood by neighbourhood: could anyone do better elsewhere at the going prices?
- Prices are set by the supply rule, not by you. Work out the prices from your split before you compute utilities.
- Nothing is graded and there is no ranking. Three attempts is plenty if the first one is a guess you can learn from.

## Think about

Who ends up in the clean neighbourhoods when preferences differ, and what does the price difference between two neighbourhoods measure? Whose willingness to pay does it reflect? We read the result against the housing-market studies of the lecture, and the question returns in Week 11.
