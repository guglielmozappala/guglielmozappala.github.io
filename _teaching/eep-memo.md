The policy project is the centrepiece of your coursework. It asks you to do what an environmental economist does for a living: take a real, contested environmental problem, diagnose it with economic tools, weigh the policies on the table, and write a recommendation that a busy decision-maker could act on.

You produce two linked deliverables: a **written policy memo** of 3,000 to 4,000 words, and a **20-minute oral presentation** of that memo followed by 10 minutes of questions. Together they are worth 45% of your final grade (memo 30%, presentation 15%). The remainder is the written exam (50%) and active class participation (5%). The project is done in teams; team size is set by enrolment and announced when teams are formed at the end of Week 4 (2 October).

## Objectives

By completing the project you show that you can:

- identify and scope a genuine environmental problem and state why the market fails;
- apply at least one theoretical framework from the course (externalities, public goods, the commons, Pigouvian pricing, the equi-marginal principle, instrument choice under uncertainty, incidence, valuation, benefit-cost analysis);
- bring in empirical evidence, naming the research design or valuation method behind every number you cite;
- compare at least two policy instruments on efficiency, cost-effectiveness, distribution and political feasibility;
- reach a reasoned, defensible recommendation and communicate it clearly, in writing and out loud.

## Deliverables at a glance

| Deliverable | What it is | Weight or status |
|---|---|---|
| Project proposal | One page: topic, the question, the two or more instruments you will compare, two or three sources | Required gate, not separately graded |
| Office-hours check-in | A short progress conversation; bring a draft outline | Required gate |
| Written memo | 3,000 to 4,000 words: problem, analysis, instruments, recommendation | 30% (team grade) |
| Oral presentation | 20 minutes plus 10 of questions, in the final sessions; equal speaking time | 15% (individual grade) |

The proposal and the check-in are mandatory milestones: you must clear them to remain eligible to submit the memo, and they count toward the participation grade.

## The written memo

**Deadline: Sunday 22 November 2026 at 23:59**, as a single PDF, one submission per team. Late submission costs one point on the 0 to 31 scale per started hour of delay; the first hour starts at 00:00, so a memo submitted at 00:00 has already lost one point. Submission instructions, including the required file name, follow on Blackboard; submissions that do not observe them lose one further point. Extensions only in advance, for a documented reason.

**The spirit of a memo.** A policy memo is not an essay and not a literature review. It is a decision document. It leads with the recommendation, justifies it with economics and evidence, and is honest about the trade-offs and about what we do not know. Imagine the reader is an adviser at the Italian Ministry of the Environment, at DG CLIMA, in a regional government, or in the sustainability office of a firm: intelligent, time-poor, not necessarily an economist.

**Required content.** Your memo must:

1. identify a real environmental problem and explain the underlying market failure;
2. apply at least one theoretical tool from the course to frame it;
3. use empirical evidence, and for every estimate name the design or method that produced it (difference-in-differences, panel fixed effects, hedonic regression, a valuation study, a benefit-cost or social-cost-of-carbon calculation);
4. evaluate at least two distinct policy instruments (a Pigouvian tax against cap-and-trade, a standard against a subsidy, a tradable-permit market against command-and-control, information disclosure against a price);
5. deliver a clear recommendation, with attention to who wins and who loses and to political and administrative feasibility.

**Suggested structure.**

| Section | Contents (indicative length) |
|---|---|
| Title and executive summary | The problem, your recommendation, the headline reason, in about 150 words a reader absorbs in one minute |
| Problem and context | What is happening, where, to whom, and why the market fails (about 500 words) |
| Economic framework | The theory that organises the problem; a diagram is welcome (about 500 words) |
| Evidence | The numbers that size the problem and the likely effects, each with its named design or method (about 700 words) |
| Instrument analysis | Your two or more instruments compared on efficiency, cost-effectiveness, distribution, feasibility (about 1,000 words) |
| Recommendation | What to do, why it dominates, caveats and unknowns (about 500 words) |
| References | Full citations, not counted in the word limit |

**Length and formatting.** 3,000 to 4,000 words excluding references, figures, tables and any appendix. A4, 11 to 12 pt body font, 1.5 line spacing, numbered pages, names and title on page 1. Up to three figures or tables in the body; anything further goes in an appendix the grader may, but need not, read. A consistent citation style; cite the source of every number and of every claim that is not your own reasoning.

## The oral presentation

- 20 minutes, then 10 minutes of questions from the instructor and your peers, in Weeks 11 and 12 (27 November and 4 December).
- Cover the same arc as the memo, ruthlessly prioritised: problem, the economics, the instrument comparison, the recommendation. Do not try to say everything.
- Aim for roughly 12 to 16 slides. Lead with the recommendation, let the evidence support it, finish on the trade-offs.
- All members present for an equal share of the 20 minutes, and everyone should be able to field questions on any part.
- The presentation is graded individually: each member receives their own mark, so plan the split so that everyone speaks for an equal amount of time.

## Choosing a topic

You have free choice of any real environmental challenge; the most engaged projects come from there. A good topic is specific rather than a whole field ("a low-emission zone for Milan" beats "air pollution"), real and current, evidence-bearing (numbers exist, and studies you can name a design for), and instrument-rich (at least two credible policies could address it).

If you are stuck, any of these works:

1. Extending the EU ETS to maritime shipping, or designing ETS2 for road transport and buildings.
2. The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism: does it stop leakage, and at what cost?
3. A congestion charge or low-emission zone for an Italian city (Milan's Area C and Area B, Rome, Naples).
4. Reducing nitrate and agricultural run-off in the Po Valley.
5. Plastic-packaging policy: the Italian plastic tax, deposit-return, extended producer responsibility.
6. Saving water in a drought-prone basin (the Po, Sicily): pricing against quotas.
7. Subsidising heat pumps and building retrofits: the lessons of the Italian *Superbonus 110%*.
8. Protecting a natural asset (the Venice lagoon, an Alpine glacier, a Natura 2000 site): valuation and financing.
9. Offshore wind siting in the Adriatic: external costs, benefits, and instrument design.
10. Whether the EU Taxonomy curbs greenwashing or merely relabels it.

## Assessment

The memo and the presentation are each graded on the 0 to 31 scale used for every component of the course: 30 is full marks and the 31st point rewards work that goes past what the rubric can score. The rubrics below score each deliverable out of 100 rubric points; that score converts to the 0 to 30 grade, the 31st point is added where earned, and any late penalty applies after the conversion. The memo mark is shared by the whole team; the presentation mark is individual.

**Rubric A: written memo (out of 100; 30% of the course grade)**

| Criterion | Points | Looking for |
|---|:---:|---|
| Problem definition and motivation | 15 | Real, specific, market failure made explicit |
| Economic framework | 20 | Correct, relevant theory, used to reason rather than decorate |
| Evidence and methods | 20 | Apt numbers; every estimate's design or method named |
| Instrument analysis (two or more) | 25 | Genuine comparison on efficiency, cost, distribution, feasibility |
| Recommendation and feasibility | 10 | Clear, justified, honest about trade-offs and unknowns |
| Writing, structure and referencing | 10 | Memo discipline; clean prose; sourced claims; within length |

**Rubric B: oral presentation (out of 100, scored per presenter; 15% of the course grade)**

| Criterion | Points | Looking for |
|---|:---:|---|
| Problem and question framing | 20 | The audience grasps the stakes in the first two minutes |
| Analysis and evidence | 25 | The right economics; numbers shown with their source or design |
| Instrument comparison and recommendation | 25 | The trade-off is clear and the recommendation follows from it |
| Slides and time management | 15 | Clean, legible slides; finishes on time |
| Handling questions | 15 | Thoughtful, honest answers that engage the question |

**What the bands mean**

| Score | Standard |
|---|---|
| 31st point | Awarded on top of a 90 to 100 score for a deliverable that does something the rubric cannot score: evidence you produced yourself, or an instrument-design argument the course did not hand you |
| 90 to 100 | Outstanding. Publishable-quality reasoning; a recommendation an expert reader would take seriously. The lowest band that converts to a 30 |
| 75 to 89 | Strong. Correct economics, well-sourced evidence, a real instrument comparison, clear writing |
| 60 to 74 | Solid. Meets all requirements with some gaps in depth, sourcing, or the comparison |
| 50 to 59 | Sufficient. Requirements largely met but the analysis is thin or claims are under-sourced |
| Below 50 | Insufficient. A requirement missing (fewer than two instruments, no named evidence), or serious errors |

If you foresee a contribution problem in your team, raise it at the Week 9 or 10 check-in, not after submission.

## Academic integrity and AI

All submitted work must be substantially your own. Cheating, plagiarism, or facilitating either is grounds for failure of the course. Cite every source; quoting or paraphrasing without attribution is plagiarism.

Generative AI tools may be used for research and drafting assistance on the memo only. If you use them, the submitted text must be substantially your own, and you include a short note disclosing how AI was used ("used to brainstorm instrument options and to copy-edit the executive summary", for example). The economic reasoning, the choice and comparison of instruments, and the recommendation must be your analysis. AI may not be used during the exam.

## A few tips

- **Narrow early.** The most common mistake is a topic three sizes too big. The proposal is where you cut it down.
- **Name the design.** "Emissions fell 10%" is weak; "a difference-in-differences study (Bayer and Aklin 2020) estimates that the EU ETS cut emissions by about 3.8%" is the standard.
- **Make the comparison fair.** Evaluate your instruments against the same criteria. A recommendation only persuades if the alternative was given a real hearing.
- **Distribution matters.** Who pays, and who benefits? A policy that is efficient but politically dead is not a good recommendation. Recall the *gilets jaunes*.
- **Lead with the answer.** Put your recommendation in the executive summary and on the first slide, then spend the rest earning it.

**Before you submit**

- A real problem, with the market failure stated.
- At least one course theory applied to reason about it.
- Evidence used, with every number's method or design named.
- At least two instruments compared on the same criteria.
- A clear recommendation, with trade-offs and unknowns acknowledged.
- 3,000 to 4,000 words; at most three figures or tables in the body; references complete.
- The AI-use note if applicable; one PDF, named and submitted exactly per the instructions, on time.
