The course meets on Fridays, 8:30 to 11:45. Each week is one three-hour session on a single topic. There is no class on 18 September and 23 October. The schedule is tentative: dates and topics may be adjusted as the course progresses, and changes are announced on Blackboard. Required readings are done before the session; K is Kolstad, *Environmental Economics*, 2nd edition. Slides are posted on Blackboard the morning before each class.

## Part I. Foundations

### Week 1 (4 September): Why study environmental economics?

*Concepts.* Course overview; the scope of environmental problems; why markets fail for environmental goods; the economist's approach to policy.

*Readings.*
- K Ch. 1 and 2.
- Fullerton, D. and Stavins, R. (1998). How economists see the environment. *Nature*, 395(6701): 433-434.

### Week 2 (11 September): Benefits, costs, efficiency, and externalities

*Concepts.* Supply, demand and market equilibrium; consumer and producer surplus; Pareto efficiency and the First Welfare Theorem; Kaldor-Hicks; the equi-marginal principle; negative and positive externalities, marginal damage, the social optimum, deadweight loss from unpriced pollution.

*Readings.*
- K Ch. 3 and 4; K Ch. 5 (sections on externalities).

*Media.*
- ["Should the Lawns in Vegas Stay in Vegas"](https://www.npr.org/2021/09/02/1033748480/should-the-lawns-in-vegas-stay-in-vegas), *The Indicator from Planet Money*, NPR (podcast).

## Part II. Market failures

### Week 3 (25 September): Public goods and the commons

*Concepts.* Rivalry and excludability, the Samuelson condition, voluntary provision and free-riding, common-pool resources, open-access equilibrium, the tragedy of the commons.

*Readings.*
- K Ch. 5 (sections on public goods, open access and common property).
- Hardin, G. (1968). The tragedy of the commons. *Science*, 162(3859): 1243-1248.

*Recommended.*
- Stavins, R. N. (2011). The problem of the commons: Still unsettled after 100 years. *American Economic Review*, 101(1): 81-108.

### Week 4 (2 October): Topic to be confirmed

The topic of this session is announced on Blackboard once the schedule of Weeks 4 to 12 has been revised. Project teams are formed by the end of this week.

## Part III. Correcting market failures: rights, rules, and prices

### Week 5 (9 October): Property rights, the Coase theorem, and environmental standards

*Room N20, Via Sraffa 13.* **Game 1, [Coase bargaining](/teaching/eep/games/coase/), is played in this session.**

*Concepts.* Coase theorem, property rights, bargaining, transaction costs, limits of Coasian approaches; command-and-control regulation, uniform standards and their cost-ineffectiveness.

*Readings.*
- K Ch. 13, Section I; K Ch. 11.
- Coase, R. H. (1960). The problem of social cost. *Journal of Law and Economics*, 3: 1-44. (Sections I to IV.)

### Week 6 (16 October): Emissions taxes

*Concepts.* Marginal damage, marginal abatement cost, the optimal Pigouvian tax, cost-effectiveness with heterogeneous firms, prices versus quantities under uncertainty (Weitzman, introductory treatment).

*Readings.*
- K Ch. 12, Sections I to III.
- Andersson, J. J. (2019). Carbon taxes and CO2 emissions: Sweden as a case study. *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*, 11(4): 1-30.

*Recommended.*
- K Ch. 15 (prices and quantities under uncertainty).
- Weitzman, M. L. (1974). Prices vs. quantities. *Review of Economic Studies*, 41(4): 477-491.

### Week 7 (30 October): Tradable permits and the emissions trading simulation

**Game 2, [permit trading](/teaching/eep/games/permits/), takes the whole session.** Students trade as firms in two parallel markets, then amend the market's rules and trade under the rule they wrote. The data the room produces are analysed in Week 8.

*Concepts.* Tradable permits, initial allocation (grandfathering versus auctioning), market clearing and the equilibrium permit price, price collars and cost-containment mechanisms.

*Readings.*
- K Ch. 13, Section II.
- Goulder, L. H. and Parry, I. W. H. (2008). Instrument choice in environmental policy. *Review of Environmental Economics and Policy*, 2(2): 152-174.

*Recommended.*
- Schmalensee, R. and Stavins, R. N. (2017). Lessons learned from three decades of experience with cap and trade. *Review of Environmental Economics and Policy*, 11(1): 59-79.
- Greenstone, M., Pande, R., Ryan, N. and Sudarshan, A. (2025). Can pollution markets work in developing countries? Experimental evidence from India. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 140(2): 1003-1060.

*Media.*
- ["A Status Report on Global Emissions Trading"](https://www.resources.org/resources-radio/status-report-global-emissions-trading-stephanie-la-hoz-theuer/), with Stephanie La Hoz Theuer, *Resources Radio*, RFF (podcast).

## Part IV. Policy evaluation in practice

### Week 8 (6 November): Evaluating environmental policy, from our own market to the EU ETS

*Concepts.* The Week 7 simulation as an experiment: treatment and control, difference-in-differences, threats to inference; the EU Emissions Trading System in practice (Phases I to IV, the Market Stability Reserve, carbon leakage, CBAM, Fit for 55) and the empirical evidence on its effects.

*Readings.*
- Bayer, P. and Aklin, M. (2020). The European Union emissions trading scheme reduced CO2 emissions despite low prices. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 117(16): 8804-8812.
- Aldy, J. E., Krupnick, A. J., Newell, R. G., Parry, I. W. H. and Pizer, W. A. (2010). Designing climate mitigation policy. *Journal of Economic Literature*, 48(4): 903-934.

## Part V. Valuing the environment

### Week 9 (13 November): Valuing the environment, revealed and stated preference

**Game 3, [hedonic sorting](/teaching/eep/games/hedonics/), is played in this session.**

*Concepts.* Total economic value, willingness to pay and to accept, use and non-use values; hedonic pricing and sorting; value of statistical life and travel cost (overview); contingent valuation, choice experiments, hypothetical bias; instrumental and intrinsic value of biodiversity.

*Readings.*
- K Ch. 7; K Ch. 8, Sections I to IV(E); K Ch. 10.
- Davis, L. W. (2004). The effect of health risk on housing values: Evidence from a cancer cluster. *American Economic Review*, 94(5): 1693-1704.
- Portney, P. R. (1994). The contingent valuation debate: Why economists should care. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 8(4): 3-17.

*Recommended.*
- K Ch. 8, Section IV(F); Ch. 9 (value of statistical life; travel cost and defensive expenditures).
- Frank, E. and Sudarshan, A. (2024). The social costs of keystone species collapse: Evidence from the decline of vultures in India. *American Economic Review*, 114(10): 3007-3040. (Introduction.)
- Frank, E. (2024). The economic impacts of ecosystem disruptions: Costs from substituting biological pest control. *Science*, 385(6713): eadg0344.
- Bilal, A., Pinardon-Touati, N. and Walsh, C. (2026). The intrinsic valuation of biodiversity loss. NBER Working Paper 35592. (Introduction and conclusion.)

## Part VI. Climate change: damages, justice, and finance

### Week 10 (20 November): Climate damages, discounting, and the social cost of carbon

*Concepts.* Identifying causal effects of climate on economic outcomes, dose-response functions, adaptation; benefit-cost analysis and the social discount rate; integrated assessment models; SCC estimation and policy use.

*Readings.*
- K Ch. 6 (benefit-cost analysis and discounting).
- Auffhammer, M. (2018). Quantifying economic damages from climate change. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 32(4): 33-52.
- Carleton, T. and Greenstone, M. (2022). A guide to updating the US government's social cost of carbon. *Review of Environmental Economics and Policy*, 16(2): 196-218.

*Recommended.*
- Dell, M., Jones, B. F. and Olken, B. A. (2014). What do we learn from the weather? The new climate-economy literature. *Journal of Economic Literature*, 52(3): 740-798.
- Carleton, T. A. and Hsiang, S. M. (2016). Social and economic impacts of climate. *Science*, 353(6304): aad9837.
- Nordhaus, W. (2017). Revisiting the social cost of carbon. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 114(7): 1518-1523.
- Greenstone, M., Kopits, E. and Wolverton, A. (2013). Developing a social cost of carbon for US regulatory analysis. *Review of Environmental Economics and Policy*, 7(1): 23-46.
- Bento, A. M., Gillingham, K. T., Jacobsen, M. R., Knittel, C. R., Leard, B., Linn, J., Rapson, D. S., Sallee, J. M., van Benthem, A. A. and Whitefoot, K. S. (2026). Correcting the logic behind the 2026 US vehicle emissions standard rollback. *Science*, 393(6814): 869-871.

*Media.*
- ["Social Cost of Carbon"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BNdS1o2GoPQ), Max Auffhammer (video lecture).
- ["Planetary Management"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KsHGPSUH7J0), Sol Hsiang (video lecture).
- ["Discount Rates: A Boring Thing You Should Know About (With Otters!)"](https://grist.org/article/discount-rates-a-boring-thing-you-should-know-about-with-otters/), David Roberts, *Grist* (blog).

### Week 11 (27 November): Distributional impacts, incidence, and environmental justice. Presentations, round 1

*Concepts.* Who bears the burden of pollution and carbon pricing? Incidence analysis, environmental justice, distributional implications of policy design. The second part of the session hosts the first round of project presentations.

*Readings.*
- K Ch. 6, Section II (incidence).
- Banzhaf, S., Ma, L. and Timmins, C. (2019). Environmental justice: The economics of race, place, and pollution. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 33(1): 185-208.
- Grainger, C. A. and Kolstad, C. D. (2010). Who pays a price on carbon? *Environmental and Resource Economics*, 46(3): 359-376.

*Recommended.*
- Jowers, K., Ma, L. and Timmins, C. D. (2026). Racial dynamics of federal property buyouts in flood-prone areas. NBER Working Paper 35726. (Introduction.)

### Week 12 (4 December): Climate finance, transition risk, and green bonds. Presentations, round 2

*Concepts.* Physical and transition risk, green bonds and the EU Taxonomy, ESG investing, SFDR and TCFD, information and market pricing of climate risk; nature and biodiversity risk and the instruments that finance conservation. The second part of the session hosts the second round of project presentations, followed by course synthesis and exam guidance.

*Readings.*
- Bolton, P., Despres, M., Pereira da Silva, L. A., Samama, F. and Svartzman, R. (2020). The green swan: Central banking and financial stability in the age of climate change. BIS. (Selected sections.)
- Giglio, S., Kelly, B. and Stroebel, J. (2021). Climate finance. *Annual Review of Financial Economics*, 13: 15-36.

*Recommended.*
- Giglio, S., Kuchler, T., Stroebel, J. and Wang, O. (2025). Nature and biodiversity loss: A research agenda for financial economics. NBER Working Paper 34286. Forthcoming, *Journal of Finance: Insights and Perspectives*.
- Garel, A., Romec, A., Sautner, Z. and Wagner, A. F. (2024). Do investors care about biodiversity? *Review of Finance*, 28(4): 1151-1186.

*Media.*
- ["Pricing Climate Risk in the Markets"](https://www.resources.org/resources-radio/pricing-climate-risk-markets-robert-litterman/), with Robert Litterman, *Resources Radio*, RFF (podcast).

## Student presentations (Weeks 11 and 12)

Teams present their policy memos during the final two sessions, about two hours per session. Each team has 20 minutes to present and 10 minutes of questions from the instructor and peers; all members present for an equal share of the time and are graded individually. Remaining time in Week 12 goes to course synthesis and exam guidance: key takeaways, open questions, and the policy debates we leave unresolved.
